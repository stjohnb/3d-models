Garden climbing frame

The design record for the climbing-frame model. It is built from pages/climbing-frame/ in the 3d-models repository, where the scripts that draw its figures and run its checks sit in src/, and CI publishes it privately on every push to main and every pull request (issue clw_01M5CSM5JY3EFGNM1VXC6EXEGM).

Issue clw_01M5CCN6MVAVKNHS7807JEQQJK shipped on 20 October 2026 as PR #27 (6c08d91); the model is in the gallery as climbing-frame. This page holds the agreed geometry, the follow-up (cross beams, monkey bars, real timber), the climbing wall on the deck end and the rope net up the swing end, in the order the issues were filed. The cross-beam issue merged as PR #28; the climbing wall and net, filed as clw_01M5CMH1MTPKEK6YHW1ZP3DG97, merged as PR #30. The swing end as an A-frame, kept alongside the cube, is filed as clw_01M5CSGZJ2ZNBGSHBHE31PD9J4 and drawn below.

Hazards to accept or design out. The monkey bars hang over the swing arcs, so a child on the bars is in the path of both swings; one activity at a time, or unclip the seats when the bars are in use. The rungs are at 2.3 m: a child starts from the deck, 750 mm below the first rung, and a 1.0–1.1 m child hanging has their feet 0.9–1.0 m off the ground, so the bay needs an impact surface such as bark or rubber mulch, which the swings need anyway. Alternative not drawn: a single 100 × 150 beam at Y=1000 carrying both swings, with the monkey bars as a separate pair of lighter rails. It keeps the swings exactly central but adds a third beam and puts both swings on one member again.

Third issue clw_01M5CMH1MTPKEK6YHW1ZP3DG97: climbing wall and rope net

Filed 20 October 2026: dashboard. Planned and refined the same day; merged as PR #30. It also carries the two default changes below: rung pitch 300 with six rungs, and swing 2 at X=1800. The timber ladder alternative is out of scope.

Once the swings hang from the cross beams, the two 2 m end faces carry nothing. The front and rear faces are not free: the slide and ladder use them at the deck end and both swing arcs pass out through them in the swing bay. So the two ends get the new activities.

Climbing wall, X=0 end. Horizontal 44 × 94 planed boards screwed to the outer faces of the two X=0 corner posts, with a 44 × 94 stile at Y=1000 behind them from a ground bearer to the deck rim, so each board spans 1000 not 2000. Fourteen boards from Z=100 to 1494 with 6 mm gaps (under the 8 mm finger-trap limit) bring the top board flush with the deck top, so a child tops out straight onto the deck. Nine to twelve bolt-on holds on a rough 450 × 400 grid, through-bolted M10 with backing washers; the bottom row at about 350 so a small child can start. Falls are onto the ground outside the frame, from at most the deck height, so the impact surface extends 1.5 m past the X=0 face. The wall also closes one side of the den under the deck.

Rope net up the swing end, X=2500. A bought 1.5 × 2.0 m cargo net, 16 mm polypropylene or poly-hemp rope on a 250–300 mm square mesh, hung from the underside of the 94 × 145 far side rail on six M10 eye bolts at 300 mm centres (Y=250 to 1750, centred under the cross beams) and tied at the bottom to a new 94 × 94 bearer bolted between the two X=2500 posts at Z 150–250. That gives 1905 mm of face for a 2.0 m net, so it hangs with about 100 mm of slack, which a climbing net wants; the bearer keeps the bottom from walking and doubles as a first step. From the top of the net a child reaches up and over the rail to the first bar at X=2250, 250 mm inboard. Nothing sits behind the net below the rail, so the mesh is clear to grip.

Tying the net yourself. Six verticals and seven horizontals on a 300 mm grid make 42 crossings. Allowing 300 mm of each rope per knot and 600 mm at each end for the hitches, that is about 58 m of 16 mm rope, so buy 70–90 m. Two ways to make the crossings that cannot slide: tie an overhand knot in both ropes together at each crossing (a fist-sized ball that doubles as a foothold, no skill needed, uses the most rope), or tie a stopper knot in each vertical at grid height and clove-hitch the horizontal around the vertical just above it, finished with an extra half hitch (neater, and the stoppers set the mesh for you). Netting books use the sheet bend or Carrick bend, but those suit nets built from short pieces and are easy to tie wrong; Animated Knots warns the tails must sit diagonally opposite or the knot works loose. Lay the grid out in chalk on a patio or peg it on the lawn, use a 300 mm plywood gauge, tie one test square first to see how much rope a knot eats, leave tails of at least 160 mm and melt or whip the ends. Top ends go through the eye bolts with a bowline or round turn and two half hitches, bottom ends round the bearer the same way after the net has hung with some weight on it for a day, because polypropylene stretches. Knots roughly halve a rope's strength; 16 mm polypropylene breaks near 2.9 t, so that is not the worry. Re-check the knots after the first month and keep the mesh at 250–300 mm so it stays out of the entrapment band after stretch.

The mid stile is what makes 44 × 94 boards work; without it the boards need to be 32 × 150 or ply. Boards win over ply outdoors: no edge delamination, same stock as the ladder stiles, and a slatted wall reads better in the model. The wall is sized for children; an adult on one hold is over the limit, as is an adult hanging from a hardwood rung. If adults will use the bars (or the ladder alternative), make every rung galvanised tube. The net's bearer at 1.8 m span sees about 1 kN of tie-down pull, 30% of a 94 × 94 C16's capacity. The rail already carries both beam ends; six eye bolts and a couple of children add little. Not an engineer's check.

Two things to change in the filed issue's defaults, cheaply, in this next issue. First, a 250 mm rung pitch leaves 215 mm between rungs, inside the 89–230 mm head-entrapment band that EN 1176-1 and the US CPSC guidance both avoid for rigid openings above 600 mm; 300 mm pitch gives 265 mm and still fits six rungs at X=750 to 2250. The filed issue makes pitch and count parameters, so this is a default change, not a redesign. Second, swing 2's seat edge at X=2095 is 405 mm from the end face where the ladder is, under the 450 mm domestic guide; moving swing 2 to X=1800 gives 480 mm and the two seats stay 600 mm apart in Y on their own beams. Both are parameter values the planner can carry into the next issue without touching the filed plan.

Hazards. A child on the net is 405–480 mm from a swinging seat 2: same rule as the bars, one activity at a time. The top of the net is at 2.15 m with an open fall to the ground, so the impact surface wraps round the X=2500 end as well, and a net makes it easy to climb onto the top rail and beams, which nothing stops. Net mesh: the US CPSC flags flexible openings with a perimeter of 430–710 mm (a 108–178 mm square) as a head-entrapment risk; a 250–300 mm mesh (1000–1200 mm perimeter) is well clear, so avoid the fine 110 mm "scramble" meshes, and keep the net tensioned so cells do not close up. EN 1176-11 covers nets for public playgrounds; its mesh limits were not retrieved. The climbing wall tops out at 1.5 m onto a deck with no guard rail, which is the shipped decision; a 44 × 94 kick board along the deck's X=0 edge would stop a knee-first arrival skidding off the far side, and is a one-line addition if wanted.

Model parameters this would add: climbing_wall (boolean, default true), wall_board_w 94, wall_board_t 44, wall_gap 6, wall_base_z 100, hold_count 9; end_net (boolean, default true), net_width 1500, net_mesh 300, bearer_z 150; end_ladder (boolean, default false) with end_ladder_pitch 300 and end_ladder_rungs 7 as the alternative on the same face. At 1:20 the boards are 4.7 mm wide and the gaps 0.3 mm, so the model prints the wall as one 2.2 mm slab with holds as 4 × 2 mm bumps and the gaps as surface grooves only when the printer resolution allows; rungs stay print-size features like the existing ladder rungs. The net is a fifth renderable, climbing_net, printed flat like the swing: a 75 × 95 mm lattice of print-size rods on a 15 mm grid, glued to the end face under the rail. Both pieces are outside the posts, so the frame STL still prints standing on its feet.

Fourth issue clw_01M5CSGZJ2ZNBGSHBHE31PD9J4: A-frame swing end, kept alongside the cube

Filed 20 October 2026: dashboard. Builds on PR #30 (climbing wall and net). The owner wants to view both structures, so the cube-ended frame, net and assembly stay as they are and the A-frame version is added as three more renderables (climbing_frame_aframe, climbing_net_aframe, climbing_assembly_aframe) behind a library switch aframe_end that is not a customizer parameter.

The swing end stops being the far wall of a box. The two far-end corner posts become legs that rise from the same ground corners and lean inwards at 17° to meet under the ends of the two cross beams, so the end face is an A with the rope net filling it. The deck end keeps its tower (X 0–708, four posts, deck, slide, ladder, climbing wall) and the two cross beams run from the tower's divider rail to the A. The side top rails along the swing bay and the two knee braces at the far end go, because there is nothing at the far corners for them to land on: the structure is the classic tower-and-swing-arm shape, a cube at one end and an A at the other, inside the same 2611 × 2000 footprint.

Reading of "A-frame": the A in the end view, legs splayed along Y, which is how swing frames are built and what resists the swings' pull. The other reading, raking the end outward along X so the net lies on a slope, pushes the leg feet 620–840 mm past the 2.5 m limit at a 70–75° net; raking inward instead shortens the beams and loses swing 2. So the net stays vertical, as in the filed issue.

Tying the trapezoid. Horizontals run leg to leg and hitch round each leg, or through an eye bolt in its inner face, so they cannot ride up the rake. Verticals run straight up from the bearer and tie off to the leg where they meet it; the V between a vertical strand and the leg points upward, which is the safe direction (a body slides down out of it, not into it), and the leg crosses the horizontals at 74°, well clear of the 60° V-opening limit. Twenty-two crossings, tied as before with two-rope overhands or stopper plus clove hitch. Rope: 7.3 m of horizontals, 6.2 m of verticals, 300 mm per rope per knot and 600 mm per hitch, about 40 m in all, so one 30 m coil is not enough and two are: 60 m of 16 mm polypropylene, £25. A bought rectangular net no longer fits; a made-to-order trapezoid is possible (O'Rourke, Huck) but the hand-tied route is the natural one here. The net laces to the legs along both sloping sides, so it is held on all four edges and stays taut, which a net hung from a rail with free sides never is.

Fifth issue clw_01M5D3V4YRPHZ6FYTS2C4DRNSM: slide bay sized for a real slide

Slide bay. The bay was sized from a nominal 500 deck strip, so the corner and divider posts left only 409 mm clear and the 500 mm chute sat flush with the corner post, 48 mm off centre; the ladder had the same offset. Real 3 m wave slides for a 1.5 m deck are 480–506 mm wide at the top (Koala Play 480, DIY Play 485, Play Away Days 491, KBT and Big Game Hunters 495, Caledonia Play 500, Garden Games and Active Garden 506), fixed by screwing the top lip to the deck edge. Decision of 20 October 2026: the opening is 520 mm clear, which passes every one of them with at least 7 mm a side. The divider posts move to a 661 centreline, the deck is 614 long, the slide and ladder are centred at X 104–604, and the swing bay keeps its clearances, so the frame grows to 2611 mm. The earlier 2.5 m limit was relaxed for this.

With the posts concreted in, as planned, overturning is carried by the embedded posts and the top rails acting as a stiff lid, so the footprint depth barely matters and 1.5 m is acceptable. Each 300 mm × 700 mm collar weighs about 120 kg before counting the soil, well above the pull on a rear post. Use M12 coach bolts at the beam and rail joints, and add knee braces from the front posts to the side rails for stiffness under repeated swinging. A surface-mounted frame would need 2 m depth and ground anchors.

Seat spacing: EN 1176-2 (public playgrounds) wants about 560 mm from seat edge to post and 660 mm between seats at this chain length, which two seats on a 2 m bay cannot meet (325 and 450 mm here). EN 71-8 for home use is less strict. A reason not to narrow the swing bay.

Decision, 20 October: the 2.5 m width is a hard limit and the frame is for younger children, so two seats stay on the 2.5 m frame at domestic-kit spacing. Equalising the seat centres to 1125 and 1875 gives 307 mm each side instead of 225 mm to the posts. The 2.5 m limit was later relaxed to 2611 for the slide bay (fifth issue below), with the swing bay's clearances kept.

For reference: the 2.5 m frame fits two seats only at the tight end of domestic practice. Widening to 3.0 m gives 450 mm clear everywhere and adds one 100 × 150 beam length and about £20 of timber; the beam check at 2.3 m span would then need re-running. The alternative is one seat centred in the bay with 680 mm each side, plus a trapeze or rope at the other hanger pair.
